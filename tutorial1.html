Q1 Direct Proof

Prove that the sum of two even integers is always even.

Question 1: Direct Proof

Proof: Let \(a\) and \(b\) be two even integers. By definition of even numbers, there exist integers \(k\) and \(m\) such that:

\[a = 2k \quad \text{and} \quad b = 2m\]

Then:

\[a + b = 2k + 2m = 2(k + m)\]

Since \(k + m\) is an integer (closure property of integers under addition), we can write \(a + b = 2n\) where \(n = k + m\) is an integer. Therefore, \(a + b\) is even by definition. ■

Q2 Proof by Contradiction

Prove that \( \sqrt{2} \) is irrational. Generalize the proof for any prime \( p \) to say \( \sqrt{p} \) is irrational.

Question 2: Proof by Contradiction

Proof: Assume \(\sqrt{2}\) is rational. Then there exist coprime integers \(p,q\) with \(q \neq 0\) such that

\[\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}.\]

Squaring both sides gives

\[p^{2} = 2q^{2}.\]

Thus \(p^{2}\) is even, so \(p\) is even. Write \(p = 2k\) for some integer \(k\). Substituting,

\[(2k)^{2} = 2q^{2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2k^{2} = q^{2}.\]

Hence \(q\) is also even, contradicting the assumption that \(\gcd(p,q)=1\). Therefore \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

Generalization:

Let \(p\) be a prime. Assume \(\sqrt{p}\) is rational. Then \(\sqrt{p}=a/b\) for integers \(a,b\) with \(\gcd(a,b)=1\) and \(b\neq 0\). Squaring,

\[a^{2} = p\,b^{2}.\]

Thus \(p\) divides \(a^{2}\), hence \(p\) divides \(a\). Write \(a = pk\). Substituting,

\[p\,b^{2} = p^{2}k^{2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad b^{2} = pk^{2}.\]

Thus \(p\) divides \(b\) as well, contradicting \(\gcd(a,b)=1\). Therefore \(\sqrt{p}\) is irrational for every prime \(p\). ■

Q3 Proof by Contrapositive

Prove that if \( n^2 \) is even, then \( n \) is even, where \( n \) is an integer.

Question 3: Proof by Contrapositive

The contrapositive of "if \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even" is "if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^2\) is odd."

Proof: Assume \(n\) is odd. Then by definition, there exists an integer \(k\) such that:

\[n = 2k + 1\]

Squaring both sides:

\[n^2 = (2k + 1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1\]

Since \(2k^2 + 2k\) is an integer, we can write \(n^2 = 2m + 1\) where \(m = 2k^2 + 2k\) is an integer. Therefore, \(n^2\) is odd by definition. We have proven the contrapositive, which is logically equivalent to the original statement. ■

Q4 Mathematical Induction

Prove by induction that for all positive integers \( n \):

\[ 1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \]

Generalize the technique of proof for any series \( \sum_{i=1}^{n} i^k = f(n) \). Find a closed form formula for \( f(n) \). Hint: Use binomial expansion.

Question 4: Mathematical Induction

Proof (by induction): We prove that \[1+2+\cdots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\] for all \(n\in \mathbb{N}\).

Base case: For \(n=1\), \[1 = \frac{1\cdot 2}{2}\] so the statement holds.

Inductive step: Assume \[1+2+\cdots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\] for some \(n\). Then

\[1+2+\cdots+n + (n+1) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + (n+1) = \frac{n(n+1)+2(n+1)}{2} = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}.\]

Thus the statement holds for \(n+1\). By induction, the identity holds for all \(n\).

General case:

Consider \[S_k(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} i^k.\] Using the binomial identity \[(m+1)^{k+1} - m^{k+1} = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \binom{k+1}{j} m^j,\] sum both sides over \(m=0\) to \(n\) to obtain a telescoping sum:

\[(n+1)^{k+1} = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \binom{k+1}{j} \sum_{m=0}^{n} m^j.\]

Noting that \(\sum_{m=0}^{n} m^j = S_j(n)\) for \(j\ge1\) and \(\sum_{m=0}^{n} 1 = n+1\), we get a linear relation among \(S_1(n),\dots,S_k(n)\), allowing recursive solution. This yields the classical Faulhaber formula: \(S_k(n)\) is a polynomial in \(n\) of degree \(k+1\) with leading term \[ \frac{n^{k+1}}{k+1}. \]

Explicitly, \[S_k(n) = \frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^j \binom{k+1}{j} B_j\, n^{\,k+1-j},\] where \(B_j\) are the Bernoulli numbers. Thus there exists a closed form polynomial \(f(n)\) of degree \(k+1\) such that \[\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^k = f(n).\] ■

Q5 Pythagorean Triplets — Three Methods

A Pythagorean triplet is a set of three positive integers \( (a, b, c) \) such that \( a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \). Prove that there are infinitely many Pythagorean triplets using three different methods.

Question 5: Infinitely Many Pythagorean Triplets

Method 1: Parametric Family

Consider the family of triplets generated by the formula: \[a = m^2 - n^2, \quad b = 2mn, \quad c = m^2 + n^2\] where \(m > n > 0\) are positive integers.

We verify this is a Pythagorean triplet:

\[\begin{aligned} a^2 + b^2 &= (m^2 - n^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 \\ &= m^4 - 2m^2n^2 + n^4 + 4m^2n^2 \\ &= m^4 + 2m^2n^2 + n^4 \\ &= (m^2 + n^2)^2 = c^2 \end{aligned}\]

Since there are infinitely many choices for \((m, n)\), there are infinitely many Pythagorean triplets.

Method 2: Scaling Construction

Start with a known Pythagorean triplet, say \((3, 4, 5)\). For any positive integer \(k\), the triplet \((3k, 4k, 5k)\) is also Pythagorean: \[(3k)^2 + (4k)^2 = 9k^2 + 16k^2 = 25k^2 = (5k)^2\] Since \(k\) can be any positive integer, there are infinitely many such triplets.

Method 3: Direct Construction from Odd Numbers

For any odd number \(n \geq 3\), define: \[a = n, \quad b = \frac{n^2 - 1}{2}, \quad c = \frac{n^2 + 1}{2}\]

We verify:

\[\begin{aligned} a^2 + b^2 &= n^2 + \left(\frac{n^2-1}{2}\right)^2 \\ &= n^2 + \frac{n^4 - 2n^2 + 1}{4} \\ &= \frac{4n^2 + n^4 - 2n^2 + 1}{4} \\ &= \frac{n^4 + 2n^2 + 1}{4} \\ &= \frac{(n^2 + 1)^2}{4} = \left(\frac{n^2+1}{2}\right)^2 = c^2 \end{aligned}\]

Since there are infinitely many odd numbers, there are infinitely many Pythagorean triplets. ■

Q6 Proof by Cases

Prove that \( n^2 + n \) is even for all integers \( n \). What would be a function that says \( f(n) \) is divisible by a prime \( p \) for all integers \( n \in \mathbb{N} \)?

Question 6: Proof by Cases

Proof: We show that \(n^2+n\) is even for all integers \(n\).

Case 1: \(n\) is even. Write \(n = 2k\). Then \[n^2+n = (2k)^2 + 2k = 4k^2 + 2k = 2(2k^2 + k),\] which is even.

Case 2: \(n\) is odd. Write \(n = 2k+1\). Then \[\begin{aligned} n^2+n &= (2k+1)^2 + (2k+1) \\ &= 4k^2 + 4k + 1 + 2k + 1 \\ &= 4k^2 + 6k + 2 \\ &= 2(2k^2 + 3k + 1), \end{aligned}\] which is even.

General divisibility function:

A function that guarantees divisibility by a prime \(p\) for all \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) is the product of \(p\) consecutive integers: \[F_p(n) = n(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+p-1).\] Among any \(p\) consecutive integers, exactly one is divisible by \(p\), hence \(p \mid F_p(n)\) for all \(n\in\mathbb{N}\). ■

Q7 Existence Proof

Prove that there exist irrational numbers \( a \) and \( b \) such that \( a^b \) is rational.

Question 7: Existence Proof

Proof: Consider \(a = \sqrt{2}\) and \(b = \sqrt{2}\). We know \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

Consider \(x = a^b = \sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}\). We have two cases:

Case 1: If \(x\) is rational, then we're done—we've found irrational numbers \(a\) and \(b\) such that \(a^b\) is rational.

Case 2: If \(x\) is irrational, consider \(y = x^{\sqrt{2}} = (\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}})^{\sqrt{2}}\). By properties of exponents: \[y = \sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}^2 = 2\] Since \(2\) is rational, we have found irrational numbers whose power is rational.

In either case, there exist irrational numbers \(a\) and \(b\) such that \(a^b\) is rational. ■

Note: This is a non-constructive existence proof.

Q8 Divisibility

Prove that for any integer \( n \), the number \( n^3 - n \) is divisible by \( 6 \). What is a function \( f(n) \) that is divisible by \( k \)?

Question 8: Divisibility

Proof: We need to show that \(6 \mid (n^3 - n)\) for any integer \(n\). Factor the expression: \[n^3 - n = n(n^2 - 1) = n(n-1)(n+1)\] This is the product of three consecutive integers.

Divisibility by 2: Among any three consecutive integers, at least one must be even. Therefore, \(2 \mid n(n-1)(n+1)\).

Divisibility by 3: Among any three consecutive integers, exactly one must be divisible by 3. Therefore, \(3 \mid n(n-1)(n+1)\).

Since 2 and 3 are coprime, \(6 \mid (n^3 - n)\) for all integers \(n\). ■

Q9 Bogus Proof

Prove that Arithmetic Mean \( \ge \) Geometric Mean.

\[ \begin{aligned} \dfrac{a+b}{2} &\stackrel{?}{\geq} \sqrt{ab} \\ a+b &\stackrel{?}{\geq} 2\sqrt{ab} \\ a^2 + 2ab + b^2 &\stackrel{?}{\geq} 4ab \\ a^2 - 2ab + b^2 &\stackrel{?}{\geq} 0 \\ (a-b)^2 &\stackrel{?}{\geq} 0 \end{aligned} \]

The last statement is true because \( a-b \) is a real number, and the square of a real number is never negative. This proves the claim.

In the above proof, identify what is wrong.

Question 9: AM-GM Inequality

The Objection: The proof is circular reasoning (begging the question). It assumes the statement to be true and derives a known truth, which does not necessarily imply the starting point holds unless the steps are reversible. In the bogus proof, the steps are marked with "?" implying uncertainty, but the logical flow isn't formally established as reversible.

Correct Proof: Since \(a\) and \(b\) are nonnegative real numbers, we know that \[(a-b)^2 \geq 0\] Expanding the left side: \[a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \geq 0\] Adding \(4ab\) to both sides: \[a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \geq 4ab\] Factoring: \[(a+b)^2 \geq 4ab\] Taking square roots (valid for \(a,b \ge 0\)): \[a + b \geq 2\sqrt{ab}\] Dividing by 2: \[\frac{a+b}{2} \geq \sqrt{ab}\] ■

Q10 Uniqueness Proof

Prove that if \( x^2 = 4 \) and \( x > 0 \), then \( x = 2 \) is the unique solution.

Question 10: Uniqueness Proof

Proof: We prove existence and uniqueness.

Existence: Substituting \(x = 2\) gives \(2^2 = 4\), and \(2 > 0\). So a solution exists.

Uniqueness: Suppose \((x')^2 = 4\) for some \(x' > 0\). Then \(x' = \pm\sqrt{4} = \pm 2\). Since \(x' > 0\), we must have \(x' = 2\). Thus, \(x = 2\) is the unique solution. ■

Logical Equivalences

Q11

Show that \( \neg(p \vee (\neg p \wedge q)) \) and \( \neg p \wedge \neg q \) are logically equivalent by developing a series of logical equivalences.

Question 11: Logical Equivalence

Show that \(\neg(p \vee (\neg p \wedge q)) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q\).

\[\begin{aligned} \neg(p \vee (\neg p \wedge q)) &\equiv \neg p \wedge \neg(\neg p \wedge q) & \text{(De Morgan's Law)} \\ &\equiv \neg p \wedge (\neg(\neg p) \vee \neg q) & \text{(De Morgan's Law)} \\ &\equiv \neg p \wedge (p \vee \neg q) & \text{(Double Negation)} \\ &\equiv (\neg p \wedge p) \vee (\neg p \wedge \neg q) & \text{(Distributive Law)} \\ &\equiv F \vee (\neg p \wedge \neg q) & \text{(Negation Law)} \\ &\equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q & \text{(Identity Law)} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \(\neg(p \vee (\neg p \wedge q)) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q\). ■

Q12

Prove that \( (p \rightarrow q) \wedge (p \rightarrow r) \) and \( p \rightarrow (q \wedge r) \) are logically equivalent using logical equivalence laws.

Question 12: Logical Equivalence

Show that \((p \rightarrow q) \wedge (p \rightarrow r) \equiv p \rightarrow (q \wedge r)\).

\[\begin{aligned} (p \rightarrow q) \wedge (p \rightarrow r) &\equiv (\neg p \vee q) \wedge (\neg p \vee r) & \text{(Conditional ID)} \\ &\equiv \neg p \vee (q \wedge r) & \text{(Distributive Law)} \\ &\equiv p \rightarrow (q \wedge r) & \text{(Conditional ID)} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \((p \rightarrow q) \wedge (p \rightarrow r) \equiv p \rightarrow (q \wedge r). ■

Q13

Show that \( \neg(p \rightarrow q) \) and \( p \wedge \neg q \) are logically equivalent by applying logical equivalence transformations.

Question 13: Logical Equivalence

Show that \(\neg(p \rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q\).

\[\begin{aligned} \neg(p \rightarrow q) &\equiv \neg(\neg p \vee q) & \text{(Conditional ID)} \\ &\equiv \neg(\neg p) \wedge \neg q & \text{(De Morgan's Law)} \\ &\equiv p \wedge \neg q & \text{(Double Negation)} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \(\neg(p \rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q\). ■

Q14

Prove that \( (p \vee q) \wedge (\neg p \vee r) \) and \( (p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge \neg p) \vee (q \wedge r) \) are logically equivalent using the distributive laws.

Question 14: Logical Equivalence

Show that \((p \vee q) \wedge (\neg p \vee r) \equiv (p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge \neg p) \vee (q \wedge r)\).

\[\begin{aligned} (p \vee q) \wedge (\neg p \vee r) &\equiv ((p \vee q) \wedge \neg p) \vee ((p \vee q) \wedge r) & \text{(Distributive)} \\ &\equiv (p \wedge \neg p) \vee (q \wedge \neg p) \vee (p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge r) & \text{(Distributive)} \\ &\equiv F \vee (q \wedge \neg p) \vee (p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge r) & \text{(Negation Law)} \\ &\equiv (q \wedge \neg p) \vee (p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge r) & \text{(Identity Law)} \\ &\equiv (p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge \neg p) \vee (q \wedge r) & \text{(Commutative)} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \((p \vee q) \wedge (\neg p \vee r) \equiv (p \wedge r) \vee (q \wedge \neg p) \vee (q \wedge r). ■

Show that \( \neg((p \vee q) \wedge (\neg p \vee \neg q)) \) and \( \neg p \wedge \neg q \) are logically equivalent by developing a series of logical equivalences.

Question 15: Logical Equivalence

Show that \(\neg((p \vee q) \wedge (\neg p \vee \neg q)) \equiv (p \leftrightarrow q)\).

Q16

Prove that \( (p \rightarrow q) \vee (q \rightarrow p) \) is a tautology using logical equivalences.

Question 16: Tautology

Show that \((p \rightarrow q) \vee (q \rightarrow p)\) is a tautology.

\[\begin{aligned} (p \rightarrow q) \vee (q \rightarrow p) &\equiv (\neg p \vee q) \vee (\neg q \vee p) & \text{(Conditional ID)} \\ &\equiv (\neg p \vee p) \vee (q \vee \neg q) & \text{(Commutative and Assoc.)} \\ &\equiv T \vee T & \text{(Negation Law)} \\ &\equiv T & \text{(Domination Law)} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \((p \rightarrow q) \vee (q \rightarrow p)\) is a tautology. ■

Q17

Show that \( \neg(p \wedge (q \vee \neg r)) \) and \( \neg p \vee (\neg q \wedge r) \) are logically equivalent by applying De Morgan's laws and other logical equivalences.

Question 17: Logical Equivalence

Show that \(\neg(p \wedge (q \vee \neg r)) \equiv \neg p \vee (\neg q \wedge r)\).

\[\begin{aligned} \neg(p \wedge (q \vee \neg r)) &\equiv \neg p \vee \neg(q \vee \neg r) & \text{(De Morgan)} \\ &\equiv \neg p \vee (\neg q \wedge \neg(\neg r)) & \text{(De Morgan)} \\ &\equiv \neg p \vee (\neg q \wedge r) & \text{(Double Negation)} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \(\neg(p \wedge (q \vee \neg r)) \equiv \neg p \vee (\neg q \wedge r). ■

Q19

Show that \( ((p \vee q) \rightarrow r) \) and \( (p \rightarrow r) \wedge (q \rightarrow r) \) are logically equivalent using logical equivalence laws.

Question 19: Logical Equivalence

Show that \(((p \vee q) \rightarrow r) \equiv (p \rightarrow r) \wedge (q \rightarrow r)\).

\[\begin{aligned} (p \vee q) \rightarrow r &\equiv \neg(p \vee q) \vee r & \text{(Conditional ID)} \\ &\equiv (\neg p \wedge \neg q) \vee r & \text{(De Morgan)} \\ &\equiv (\neg p \vee r) \wedge (\neg q \vee r) & \text{(Distributive)} \\ &\equiv (p \rightarrow r) \wedge (q \rightarrow r) & \text{(Conditional ID)} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \(((p \vee q) \rightarrow r) \equiv (p \rightarrow r) \wedge (q \rightarrow r). ■

Q20

Prove that \( \neg(p \leftrightarrow q) \) and \( (p \wedge \neg q) \vee (\neg p \wedge q) \) are logically equivalent by developing a series of logical equivalences.

Question 20: Logical Equivalence

Show that \(\neg(p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv (p \wedge \neg q) \vee (\neg p \wedge q)\).

\[\begin{aligned} \neg(p \leftrightarrow q) &\equiv \neg((p \rightarrow q) \wedge (q \rightarrow p)) & \text{(Definition)} \\ &\equiv \neg(p \rightarrow q) \vee \neg(q \rightarrow p) & \text{(De Morgan)} \\ &\equiv (p \wedge \neg q) \vee (q \wedge \neg p) & \text{(Negation of Imp.)} \\ &\equiv (p \wedge \neg q) \vee (\neg p \wedge q) & \text{(Commutative)} \end{aligned}\]

This represents the XOR (exclusive OR) operation. ■
